Homework 6 – Statistical independence: contingency table and χ² test

1. Theory

Probability. Two events A and B are independent if P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B), equivalently P(A | B) = P(A) when P(B) > 0: knowing B does not change the probability of A. For two discrete variables X and F independence means P(X = i, F = j) = P(X = i)·P(F = j) for every pair of categories (i, j), that is, the conditional distribution of F is the same for every value of X (and the other way round).

From data. For a sample of N pairs (X, F) arranged in an r × c contingency table with counts nij: row totals ni·, column totals n·j, marginal frequencies fi· = ni·/N and f·j = n·j/N, conditional frequencies f(F=j | X=i) = nij/ni· and f(X=i | F=j) = nij/n·j. If X and F were independent, every conditional row would be close to the marginal one, and the counts would be close to the expected counts Eij = ni··n·j/N (N times the product of the marginals).

χ² test of independence. H0: X and F are independent. Statistic χ² = Σij (Oij − Eij)²/Eij. Under H0 and for large samples it is approximately χ² distributed with (r−1)(c−1) degrees of freedom (the r + c − 1 free marginals are estimated from the data). The p-value is P(χ²df ≥ observed value): the probability, if H0 holds, of a discrepancy at least this large. Reject H0 if p < α (typically 0.05). Conditions: independent observations, and expected counts mostly ≥ 5 (Cochran’s rule). Extra tools used below: standardized residuals (O − E)/√E, Cramér’s V = √(χ²/(N·min(r−1, c−1))) as a measure of strength (0 to 1), and a permutation p-value obtained by reshuffling F many times, which does not rely on the χ² approximation.

5. Interpretation

6. Association is not causality

A significant χ² means that X and F are statistically associated (dependent): the distribution of F differs across the values of X. It says nothing about why. The test is symmetric in X and F, so it cannot tell which variable acts on which. Possible explanations of an association: X influences F; F influences X (reverse causation); a third variable Z (a confounder) influences both; or chance (about α of true-independence datasets are flagged). Aggregating over a hidden variable can even reverse the direction of an association (Simpson’s paradox). Establishing causality needs more than a contingency table: randomized experiments (the investigator assigns X at random, which breaks the link with confounders), or careful causal designs and background knowledge.

Other points to keep in mind: a very large N makes even tiny, irrelevant departures from independence “significant”, so report an effect size (Cramér’s V) and not only the p-value; with small samples the test may fail to detect a real association, so a large p-value does not prove independence; and a p-value is not the probability that H0 is true. In the “training vs phishing” example, the association would be compatible with training really reducing clicks, but also with, say, more careful employees being more likely to attend training: only an experiment that assigns the training at random could separate the two explanations.
