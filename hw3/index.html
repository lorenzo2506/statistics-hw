Homework 3 – Penetration process: Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem

Model: an attacker tries to penetrate n systems one after the other; step i gives a score Xi (i.i.d.). We simulate m attackers (m independent trajectories) and study Sn, Sn/n and Sn/√n as n grows. Bernoulli: X ∈ {0,1}, P(X=1)=p. Rademacher: X ∈ {−1,+1} with probability ½ each (symmetric, zero mean).

6. Interpretation

Total score Sn. Its variance is nσ², so the spread grows like √n. In the Bernoulli case the mean grows linearly (np): the number of compromised systems out of n is about np give or take a few √(np(1−p)). In the Rademacher case the mean stays 0 and Sn is a symmetric random walk (gains and losses cancel on average) that wanders over a range of order √n without drift.

Average score Sn/n. Variance σ²/n → 0: the trajectories squeeze towards the mean μ (p for Bernoulli, 0 for Rademacher). This is the Law of Large Numbers: for every ε > 0, P(|Sn/n − μ| ≥ ε) → 0. Chebyshev only gives the bound σ²/(nε²); the real probability decays much faster (Hoeffding gives an exponential bound), as the table shows. The limit is degenerate (a constant), so this scaling hides the shape of the fluctuations.

Normalized score Sn/√n. Variance σ² for every n: this is the scaling at which the fluctuations neither explode nor vanish. In the Rademacher case Sn/√n converges in distribution to N(0,1) (CLT): the histograms approach the bell curve, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance falls roughly like 1/√n (for lattice variables) until it reaches the sampling noise of order 1/√m, the skewness stays 0 and the excess kurtosis −2/n → 0. In the Bernoulli case Sn/√n has mean √n·p → ∞, so it does not converge; only the centred and standardized version (Sn − np)/√(np(1−p)) converges to N(0,1), with a speed that depends on p (skewness (1−2p)/√(np(1−p)): small or large p converge more slowly).

Systems and attackers. The number of systems n controls the size and the relative reliability of the aggregate score: larger n means more total penetrations but a smaller relative uncertainty (≈ 1/√n), so a defender can plan for about np ± 3√(np(1−p)) compromised systems. The number of attackers m does not change the distribution of one attacker’s score; it improves our estimate of that distribution (sampling error ≈ 1/√m, visible as the KS noise floor). If the m attackers act independently, the pooled score is a sum of mn i.i.d. terms, with mean mnp and standard deviation √(mnp(1−p)), so the pooled average has variance σ²/(mn).

Limits of the model: independent systems and attackers, the same p for every step, no learning or defence reaction. Real penetration processes have dependence and heterogeneity, and then the classical LLN and CLT may need modification.
