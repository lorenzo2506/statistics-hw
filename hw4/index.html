Homework 4 – Welford’s recurrences; sampling mean and variance of hacker scores

Exercise 1 – Welford’s recurrence formulas

1.1 Derivation

Notation: x1, x2, … are the data; after k data, Mk is the mean, SSk = Σi≤k (xi − Mk)² the sum of squares, and δk = xk − Mk−1 the “surprise” of the new datum with respect to the old mean.

Mean (Knuth). Since k·Mk = (k−1)·Mk−1 + xk:

M_k = ((k-1) M_{k-1} + x_k) / k
    = M_{k-1} + (x_k - M_{k-1}) / k
    = M_{k-1} + δ_k / k                       (M_0 = 0)

Useful consequence:  x_k - M_k = δ_k - δ_k/k = δ_k (k-1)/k

Sum of squares. Expanding the square gives SSk = Σ xi² − k·Mk², hence the increment is

SS_k - SS_{k-1} = x_k² - k M_k² + (k-1) M_{k-1}²

Write M = M_{k-1} and x_k = M + δ, M_k = M + δ/k:
  x_k²    = M² + 2Mδ + δ²
  k M_k²  = k M² + 2Mδ + δ²/k
  (k-1)M² = (k-1) M²
Sum:  M² + 2Mδ + δ² - kM² - 2Mδ - δ²/k + (k-1)M² = δ² - δ²/k = δ² (k-1)/k

Since δ (x_k - M_k) = δ · δ (k-1)/k  we get the Welford formula:

  SS_k = SS_{k-1} + (x_k - M_{k-1}) (x_k - M_k)          (SS_0 = 0)

Variance. The sample variance (unbiased) is s²k = SSk/(k−1) for k ≥ 2, and the “population” (biased) one is SSk/k. From the SS recurrence:

s²_k = SS_k / (k-1) = ((k-2)/(k-1)) s²_{k-1} + δ_k² / k           (k ≥ 2)
v_k  = SS_k / k     = ((k-1)/k) v_{k-1} + ((k-1)/k²) δ_k²

In practice one updates M and SS and divides only when the variance is needed.

1.2 Code (core logic: 3 lines)

// for each new datum x, k = number of data seen so far (including x)
const delta = x - mean;        // δ_k = x_k - M_{k-1}
mean += delta / k;             // M_k  = M_{k-1} + δ_k / k
ss   += delta * (x - mean);    // SS_k = SS_{k-1} + δ_k (x_k - M_k)   (uses the NEW mean)
// afterwards:  variance = ss / (k - 1)

1.4 Why Welford? Numerical stability (optional)

The textbook one-pass formula SS = Σx² − (Σx)²/n subtracts two huge, almost equal numbers when the data have a large mean and a small spread, and loses all precision in floating point. Welford works on small differences instead.

Adding a constant to all data changes the mean but not the sum of squares, so the correct value is always the one for the data without offset.

Exercise 2 – Sampling mean and variance of the hackers’ scores

Each of m hackers attempts n systems; each attempt gets an integer score in {0, …, 10} drawn from a fixed distribution with mean μ and variance σ². For each hacker the sampling mean x̄ and the sampling variance s² of the n scores are computed with Welford’s recurrences. Then the m values of x̄ and of s² are compared with μ and σ².

2.3 Results

Conclusions

Averages versus the theory. The average of the m sampling means is very close to μ for every n, and the average of the m sampling variances s² (divisor n−1) is very close to σ²: both estimators are unbiased, E[x̄] = μ and E[s²] = σ². If the sum of squares is divided by n instead of n−1, the average is systematically lower, σ²(n−1)/n; the gap is large for small n and fades as n grows. This is Bessel’s correction: the deviations are measured from x̄, which is fitted to the same data and therefore sits closer to them than μ does, so SS/n underestimates the spread.

Variability. Each single hacker gives a noisy value. The variance of the sampling means is σ²/n, and the variance of s² is μ4/n − σ⁴(n−3)/(n(n−1)) (μ4 = fourth central moment), also of order 1/n. So increasing the number of systems n attempted by each hacker shrinks the spread of both estimators: the standard deviation falls like 1/√n (a hundred times more systems gives a ten times smaller spread). Both estimators are therefore consistent: they converge to the true parameters (law of large numbers). The histograms also show the CLT: the distribution of x̄ becomes normal and, for large n, so does that of s².

Why this matters in inferential statistics. In practice σ² and μ are unknown, and we have a single sample. Unbiasedness and consistency justify using x̄ and s² as estimates. The size of the fluctuation, the standard error σ/√n (estimated by s/√n), is what builds confidence intervals and t-tests, and it tells us how many systems we must test to reach a desired precision. The m-hacker experiment is a Monte Carlo way to see the sampling distribution of an estimator, which is a theoretical notion we can never observe directly with one dataset. Welford’s recurrences are the tool to get these estimates in a single pass, one datum at a time, with good numerical stability and constant memory.
